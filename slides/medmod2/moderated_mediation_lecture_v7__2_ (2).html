This example uses the disaster dataset from Hayes (2022) and walks through Hayes Model 1 — simple moderation — using the R package processR.

processR Example — Step 1

Conceptual Model: Frame → Justify, Moderated by Skepticism

We begin by defining our variable labels and plotting the conceptual diagram. This is the big-picture view: X affects Y, with W moderating that relationship.

library(processR)
library(lavaan)

# Fit the moderation model
fit <- lm(justify ~ frame * skeptic,
           data = disaster)

# Define labels for diagrams
labels <- list(X = "frame",
               W = "skeptic",
               Y = "justify")

# Plot conceptual model (Model 1)
pmacroModel(1, labels = labels)
What This Shows
The conceptual model places skeptic (W) as a moderator on the path from frame (X) to justify (Y). No mediator — this is pure moderation (Hayes Model 1).

Fig 1. Conceptual model — W (skeptic) moderates the X→Y path

Motivation

Why Moderated Mediation Matters for Social Work Practice

  • Universal interventions fail marginalized groups: An average treatment effect of zero can hide a strong benefit for one group and harm for another — moderated mediation reveals this
  • Mechanism ≠ universal: The same exposure can operate through completely different psychological processes depending on identity, context, or biology
  • Policy precision: Knowing W defines who needs what intervention. "Trauma-informed care works — for boys who have low social support" is actionable. "It works on average" is not.
  • Ethical obligation: Reporting only average effects can obscure differential harm. PhD-level social work researchers are obligated to report heterogeneity.
Real Example
A school-based trauma intervention showed no average effect in an RCT. Moderated mediation revealed it worked strongly for girls (through emotion regulation) but had no effect for boys. The program was being discontinued. The conditional analysis saved it — and redesigned the boys’ component.
The Precision Medicine Analogy
We don’t prescribe the same medication dose to all patients. We shouldn’t prescribe the same intervention to all children. Moderated mediation is the statistical equivalent of precision medicine.
This slide is your hook — use it before any equations. PhD students often learn the math but lose sight of WHY. Drive home: social work is ethically required to understand heterogeneity of effects. The "real example" is worth telling as a story — pause, describe the scenario, let them imagine being the researcher who almost lost the program. This is not abstract. The precision medicine analogy tends to land well — most students have heard this in health contexts. Ask: what would the equivalent look like in a trauma intervention? Who is the "patient population" we're stratifying? Common student question here: "Can't we just stratify by sex and run two separate models?" — Answer: Yes, but that's less powerful, loses precision, and doesn't give you a formal test of whether the groups differ (the interaction term IS that test). Teaser for the next 90 minutes.
Before You Run the Model

Data Requirements & Key Assumptions

  • Sample size: Conditional process models are power-hungry. For Model 7 with bootstrapping, aim for N ≥ 200. Model 59 needs N ≥ 400–500 to detect moderate quadratic indirect effects with adequate power (Fritz & MacKinnon, 2007)
  • Linearity: Assumes linear relationships between all variables. Inspect scatterplots of X–M and M–Y. Non-linearity biases interaction estimates
  • Measurement reliability: Unreliable measures attenuate interaction effects — interaction terms are among the first things eaten by measurement error
  • Multicollinearity: Interaction terms (X×W) correlate highly with their constituent variables. Check VIF after computing the product term. VIF > 10 is a problem
  • Normal errors: OLS assumptions still apply to each regression equation; bootstrapping corrects for non-normal sampling distributions of indirect effects, not for non-normal residuals
The Bootstrapping Misconception
Bootstrapping does not fix small samples — it provides more accurate CIs for the sampling distribution of a product. With N < 100, even bootstrapped CIs are unreliable. Power analysis before data collection is non-negotiable.
PROCESS Requirement
PROCESS requires complete cases by default. Missing data on any variable drops the entire row. Use multiple imputation (MICE in R or SPSS) before running PROCESS if missingness > 5%.
Students often rush to run models without checking these. Spend time here. The sample size point is critical: many dissertation proposals are underpowered for moderated mediation. Fritz and MacKinnon (2007) showed that detecting a small-to-medium indirect effect at 80% power requires N=400+. On multicollinearity: explain that this is WHY we center — not to "fix" collinearity mathematically, but to make the main effect coefficients interpretable when the interaction is in the model. The product term X×W is always correlated with X and W; centering reduces that correlation and makes a1 and a2 interpretable at meaningful values. VIF check: in R, run vif(lm(M ~ X + W + X*W)). In SPSS, collinearity diagnostics in linear regression. VIF of 10+ means your estimates for a1 and a2 are unstable — interpret only the interaction, not the main effects. Missing data: PROCESS drops incomplete cases silently. Remind students to run md.pattern() in R or Missing Value Analysis in SPSS before submitting data to PROCESS.
Before You Run the Model

Centering X and W: What, Why, and How

  • What centering is: Subtracting the mean from each variable before computing the product term (X×W). Mean-centered X and W have means of zero.
  • Why it matters: Without centering, the main effect of X (a₁) is estimated at W = 0, which may be a nonsensical or non-existent value of W. After centering, a₁ = effect of X at the mean of W — meaningful and interpretable.
  • It does NOT change the interaction: The a₃ coefficient, its SE, and p-value are identical whether you center or not. Only the main effects and intercept change.
  • Standardized vs. mean-centered: Standardizing (z-scoring) W also works — then percentiles map directly to SD units. PROCESS does this automatically if you choose standardized output.
  • Binary moderators: If W is 0/1 (e.g., sex), no centering needed. The main effect of X is simply the effect for the W=0 group.
Without centering — a₁ is X effect when W literally = 0

M = i + a₁X + a₂W + a₃(X×W) + e

With centering — a₁ is X effect at average W (interpretable)

M = i + a₁Xc + a₂Wc + a₃(Xc×Wc) + e

PROCESS Tip
PROCESS has a built-in centering option. In the dialog: Options → Mean center for products. Always enable this unless you have a specific reason not to (e.g., W has a meaningful zero like age in months from birth).
This is a slide students often gloss over and then struggle with during dissertation defense. The key insight: centering changes interpretation of main effects, not the interaction. Demonstrate this: pull up a mini dataset, run with and without centering, show that a3 is identical. The intercept and a1, a2 differ — that's exactly the point. Good analogy: a thermometer. The difference between 20°C and 25°C is the same whether you're measuring in Celsius or Fahrenheit — the scale doesn't change relative differences. Similarly, centering doesn't change the X×W interaction, it just rescales what "zero" means for the main effects. If time permits: ask students to compute by hand. If W ranges from 1–7, what does the effect of X "at W=0" even mean? Nothing interpretable. But "at the average of W" (say, W̄=4) is very meaningful. Common confusion: "Does centering hurt interpretation of the indirect effect?" Answer: No — the indirect effect is (a1+a3W)×b. With centering, at the mean of W (which is now 0), the indirect effect is a1×b — exactly the average indirect effect. Very intuitive.
Conceptual Foundation

Mediation, Moderation, and Conditional Process

  • Mediation explains the mechanism: Violence → PTSS → Delinquency. M is the “why.”
  • Moderation identifies boundary conditions: Does Sex change the strength of any path?
  • Conditional Process — both simultaneously. The mechanism itself varies across values of W.
  • Goal — demographic-aware intervention science that identifies for whom and under what conditions effects operate.
In Plain Language
W is the “lens.” The same violence exposure can produce different trauma, and that trauma can drive different behavior, depending on who we are studying.
This is the conceptual anchor for the whole class. Spend 3–4 minutes here. The diagram on the right shows the simplest moderated mediation: W points to the a-path only (that's Model 7). Use the bridge metaphor out loud: "The bridge between violence and delinquency is the trauma pathway. W — sex — changes how strong that bridge is. For boys the bridge is made of steel. For girls it's still a bridge, but maybe wood. Same violence, same destination, different load capacity." Ask: why does this matter for intervention? If trauma is the bridge, intervention targets trauma. But if the bridge is stronger for boys, your intervention needs to be heavier-duty for that group. Stress: "conditional process" is Hayes' term for what happens when mediation and moderation exist simultaneously. Some textbooks call it "moderated mediation." They are the same thing.
Key Concept

The Indirect Effect as a Function: f(W)

  • In simple mediation, the indirect effect is one number: ab
  • In moderated mediation, it becomes a function of the moderator: the indirect effect changes depending on W
  • Notation: f(W) = θM|X × θY|M
  • Think of a bridge: its strength changes as W (sex, support, context) changes
  • We probe this function at specific W values — using Pick-a-Point or Johnson-Neyman
Why it matters
A single indirect effect estimate hides who is affected most. f(W) reveals the full picture.
Example
In Model 7 (Violence → PTSS → Delinquency, moderated by Sex): f(W) = (a1 + a3W) × b. The indirect effect is different for boys and girls — not a single number but a conditional one.
Key Concept · Visualized

f(W): How the Indirect Effect Varies Across the Moderator

Reading this graph
At W=0 (female), the indirect effect is 0.23. At W=1 (male), it is 0.39. The slope of the line shows how the indirect effect changes across values of W.
How We Probe f(W)
Pick-a-Point: Evaluate f(W) at 16th, 50th, 84th percentiles of W — reporting conditional indirect effects at three representative values.

Johnson-Neyman: Find the exact value of W where f(W) transitions from non-significant to significant.

Model 7

First-Stage Moderation

W moderates the a-path only · The b-path is constant across W

Model 7 · Structural Equations

The Two Regression Equations

Mediator Equation (M model) — interaction appears HERE

M = iM + a1X + a2W + a3(X×W) + eM

Outcome Equation (Y model) — no interaction, b is constant

Y = iY + c′X + bM + eY

  • a3 is the critical interaction term — it measures how much the X→M slope changes per unit of W
  • b has no subscript — the M→Y relationship is identical for all values of W
  • The conditional indirect effect is: (a1 + a3W) × b
  • This is a linear function of W — it grows or shrinks proportionally
When to use Model 7
When your theory says W changes trauma sensitivity (how much X creates M), but that once traumatized, everyone responds to trauma with the same behavioral outcomes.
Walk through the equations slowly — write them on the board as you speak. The key pedagogical move: cover the M equation first, show that it's just a regular moderated regression. Then reveal the Y equation — notice NO interaction. Ask students: "What does it mean that b has no subscript?" It means the PTSS-to-delinquency slope is the same for everyone. Sex changes how violence creates trauma, but once you have trauma, it hurts everyone equally. Have students write down the conditional indirect effect formula: (a1 + a3W) × b. This is THE equation they must be able to derive from memory. In the b-constant model, only a1 + a3W varies. The whole story is in the a-path. Pose this: "What evidence would you need to justify Model 7 vs. Model 8?" Answer: theory. Does your theory predict that W only changes X→M, or does it also predict W changes X→Y directly? Model selection must be theoretically driven, not empirically fished.

The Bootstrap CI note is important for the dissertation students: they will be tempted to use the Sobel test because it gives a p-value. Bootstrapping sidesteps normality — we just resample and build the empirical distribution of ab. Ask: "What's the minimum number of bootstrap resamples for a dissertation?" Probing Methods

How Do We Probe Conditional Effects?

Pick-a-Point & Johnson-Neyman

Two complementary approaches to understanding where and for whom effects are significant

Probing Methods · Pick-a-Point

Pick-a-Point: Why the 16th, 50th, and 84th Percentile?

  • The problem: When W is continuous (e.g., trauma score, support scale), we cannot report effects for every possible value of W
  • The solution: Report conditional effects at three strategically chosen “representative” values
  • Mean ± 1SD rule: For a normally distributed W, the 16th percentile ≈ mean − 1SD, the 84th ≈ mean + 1SD
  • Why these? They span the most common range of W values (roughly 68% of participants fall between them) without going to extremes
  • Centered interpretation: The 50th percentile (median/mean) is the “average person” condition
Why Not Mean±1SD Directly?
Using exact percentiles is more robust when W is skewed or non-normal. PROCESS reports these automatically. For binary W (like sex), just use the two groups.

Model 8

Dual Moderation

W moderates the a-path AND the direct c′-path

Model 8 · Structural Equations

Both Equations Now Contain W

Mediator Equation (same as Model 7)

M = iM + a1X + a2W + a3(X×W) + eM

Outcome Equation — NEW: direct path is now also moderated

Y = iY + c′1X + c′2W + c′3(X×W) + bM + eY

  • Indirect f(W): (a1 + a3W) × b — the trauma mechanism varies by W
  • Direct f(W): c′1 + c′3W — the behavioral bypass also varies by W
  • Both must be reported and probed separately using pick-a-point
  • Two separate “switches” — one for the psychological mechanism, one for the direct behavioral reaction
Theoretical Implication
Model 8 says: boys are more vulnerable through the trauma pathway (a-path moderated) AND they also react more behaviorally to violence even when trauma is held constant (c′-path moderated). Two distinct sex-moderated risks.

Section 3 of 4

Model 59

Full Conditional Process

W moderates a, b, and c′ · Quadratic indirect effect · Every link is contingent

Model 59 · Structural Equations

Three Interactions Across Two Equations

Mediator Equation — Int_1 moderates the a-path

M = iM + a1X + a2W + a3(X×W) + eM

Outcome Equation — Int_2 on c′-path, Int_3 on b-path

Y = iY + c′1X + c′2W + c′3(X×W) + b1M + b2W + b3(M×W) + eY

  • The indirect effect becomes (a1 + a3W)(b1 + b3W) — a product of two linear functions of W
  • This creates a quadratic (curved) indirect effect — it accelerates rather than growing linearly
  • Int_2 (c′-path) may or may not be significant — it is tested independently
Why Quadratic?
When W moderates both stages of mediation, the two effects multiply. Small W = small×small = very small. Large W = large×large = very large. The acceleration is built into the math.

Model 59 · Quadratic f(W)

Why the Indirect Effect Accelerates (Quadratic Logic)

Indirect Effect as a Function of W

f(W) = (a1 + a3W)(b1 + b3W)

= (0.06 + 0.32W)(0.22 + 1.03W)

  • At W = 1.29 (low): (0.06+0.41)(0.22+1.33) = (0.47)(1.55) ≈ 0.73
  • At W = 2.99 (med): (0.06+0.96)(0.22+3.08) = (1.02)(3.30) ≈ 3.37
  • At W = 4.69 (high): (0.06+1.50)(0.22+4.83) = (1.56)(5.05) ≈ 7.88
  • From low to high W: 0.73 → 3.34 → 7.83 — not linear, accelerating
Practical Meaning
You cannot simply extrapolate from the average (50th) to the extremes. The acceleration means high-W individuals benefit disproportionately more than low-W individuals.
The Upward Curve
The red curve bends upward — each additional unit of W produces a larger gain in the indirect effect than the previous unit. The acceleration is caused by the multiplication of two linear functions.

Probing Methods · Johnson-Neyman

The Johnson-Neyman Technique: Why and When

  • Limitation of pick-a-point: We chose three arbitrary values of W — but significance might change somewhere between them. We are missing the transition point.
  • J-N solves this: Computes the exact value(s) of W where the effect of X on Y (or the indirect effect) transitions from non-significant to significant
  • Output: A “zone of significance” — the range of W values where the effect is statistically significant at α = .05
  • For continuous W: J-N is preferred because it does not require you to pick representative values — it finds the transition analytically
  • For indirect effects: Because bootstrapping is required, J-N is approximated using a “floodlight analysis”
In Plain Language
The J-N point (W = 2.14) is the “threshold of significance.” For participants with W above this value, the effect is significant. Below it, it is not.

Pick-a-Point vs. J-N: When to use which?
Pick-a-Point: Best when W is categorical (e.g., sex) or when theory specifies meaningful values (e.g., clinical cut-offs, policy thresholds).

Johnson-Neyman: Best when W is continuous and you want to identify the exact transition point. Especially valuable when the 16th-percentile probe is not significant — J-N tells you exactly where significance begins.
Important Limitation
J-N for indirect effects requires bootstrapping and is not analytically exact. Use the “floodlight analysis” or PROCESS with many probing values. Report with caution.
Johnson-Neyman · The Floodlight Plot

Visualizing the Zone of Significance

  • The gold vertical line is the J-N point — where the lower CI crosses zero
  • Left of J-N: the CI band straddles zero → effect is not significant
  • Right of J-N: the entire CI band is above zero → effect is significant
  • The slope of the effect line itself shows how fast the effect strengthens as W increases
  • Report: “The effect of X on Y was significant for values of W above 2.14, which represents 72.3% of the sample.”
Reporting Template
“A Johnson-Neyman analysis indicated that the effect of [X] on [Y] was statistically significant (p < .05) for values of [W] above [J-N value] (representing [%] of the sample). Below this threshold, the effect was not significant.”

Advanced Inference & Reporting

Index of Moderated Mediation, Standards, and Decision Guide

Advanced Inference

The Index of Moderated Mediation (Hayes)

  • What it is: A single statistic confirming that the indirect effect truly varies as a function of W
  • How it works: It is the coefficient(s) linking W to the indirect effect. In Model 7, it equals a3×b. In Model 59, it is more complex.
  • Rule: If the bootstrap CI of the Index excludes zero → moderated mediation is statistically confirmed
  • Why it’s needed: Significant interaction terms (a3, b3) are necessary but not sufficient — the Index is the formal test of the product
  • Always report: The Index and its CI alongside your conditional indirect effects
Key Rule
If the 95% bootstrap CI of the Index of Moderated Mediation does not include zero → moderated mediation is proven. This is your primary confirmation statistic.
Reporting Template
“The index of moderated mediation was [X], 95% CI [LL, UL]. Since this CI excludes zero, the indirect effect of [X] on [Y] through [M] significantly varied as a function of [W].”
The IMM (Index of Moderated Mediation) is Hayes' most important contribution to this literature — and the one most often missing from student write-ups. Stress: significant interaction terms (a3, b3) are NECESSARY but NOT SUFFICIENT to conclude moderated mediation. You also need the IMM. Why? Because a3 and b3 only test whether specific paths vary. The IMM tests whether the INDIRECT EFFECT — the product — varies as a function of W. In Model 7, IMM = a3 × b. Simple enough. In Model 59, the IMM is more complex (it involves a quadratic term). But the logic is the same: does the CI exclude zero? Practical tip: In PROCESS, you get the IMM automatically in the "Conditional indirect effects" section at the bottom of the output. Look for "Index of Moderated Mediation" — it will show the estimate and bootstrap CI. If you don't see it, you may need to set the right output options. Have students memorize the reporting template. This sentence should appear in every conditional process results section they ever write.
Decision Guide

Which Model Do You Need?

Model 7
  • Use when: W changes how X creates M, but not how M predicts Y
  • Example: Sex makes boys more trauma-sensitive, but trauma predicts delinquency equally for all
  • Key interaction: a3 in M equation
  • f(W) shape: Linear
  • Probe: Simple slopes of X on M by W
Model 8
  • Use when: W changes both the trauma mechanism AND the direct behavioral response
  • Example: Boys react more via trauma AND also react more directly to violence independent of trauma
  • Key interactions: a3 and c′3
  • f(W) shape: Two separate linear functions
  • Probe: Both indirect and direct effects separately
Model 59
  • Use when: W conditions every causal link — a, b, and c′
  • Example: Parent support controls how effort builds trust AND how trust creates success
  • Key interactions: a3, b3, and c′3
  • f(W) shape: Quadratic (accelerating)
  • Probe: Quadratic indirect effect table + J-N floodlight
PhD Reporting Standards

Five Steps to a Complete Results Section

  • Step 1 — Specify the Model: State the PROCESS model number, justify the choice theoretically, and identify which paths are moderated
  • Step 2 — Report Interaction Terms: State whether each interaction (Int_1, Int_2, Int_3) is significant, its coefficient, and its substantive meaning
  • Step 3 — Conditional Indirect Effects: Present estimates at the 16th, 50th, and 84th percentiles with bootstrap CIs (10,000 resamples minimum)
  • Step 4 — Johnson-Neyman Analysis: When W is continuous, report the J-N transition point and the percentage of the sample in the significant region
  • Step 5 — Index of Moderated Mediation: Report the Index and its CI as the confirmatory statistic; then translate into clinical or policy language
Bootstrapping Standard
Use 10,000 resamples for dissertation-level work. Never use the Sobel test for indirect effects — it assumes normality that products of coefficients do not satisfy.

Common Errors

The Seven Most Common Mistakes in Moderated Mediation

  • 1. Model selection without theory: Choosing Model 7 vs. 8 vs. 59 based on which one "fits better" — not on theoretical justification. This is HARKing (Hypothesizing After Results are Known).
  • 2. Ignoring the IMM: Reporting conditional indirect effects without the Index of Moderated Mediation. The IMM is the confirmatory test.
  • 3. Using Sobel test for indirect effects: The Sobel test is biased. Always use bootstrapped CIs with 10,000 resamples.
  • 4. Not centering continuous moderators: Makes main effects uninterpretable. Enable PROCESS centering option.
  • 5. Treating significant a₃ as sufficient evidence: A significant interaction on the a-path does not alone prove moderated mediation. The indirect effect CI must exclude zero.
  • 6. Forgetting to probe: Reporting Int_1 is significant and stopping. You must show the conditional indirect effects at 16th, 50th, and 84th percentiles — or the J-N analysis for continuous W.
  • 7. Proportion mediated as primary statistic: Proportion mediated can exceed 1.0 or be negative. It is a secondary descriptive, not a confirmatory test.
The Dissertation Red Flag
If a committee member asks "how did you select your PROCESS model number?" — you must have a theoretical answer ready. "I tried several and this one had the lowest AIC" is not acceptable.
This slide is gold for dissertation students — these are the exact mistakes reviewers and committee members catch. Go through them one by one and ask: "Have any of you already made this mistake in your proposal?" Let it be a bit humorous — everyone has made at least one. Error 1 is the most serious. Emphasize: the PROCESS model number must be chosen BEFORE data collection, based on your theoretical framework. If your theory says W only moderates the a-path, you run Model 7. Full stop. Error 5 catches students who read Hayes cursorily. The a3 interaction tells you the first stage varies — but moderated mediation requires the PRODUCT to vary. Even if a3 is huge, if b is near zero, the indirect effect barely changes across W. Error 7: Walk through an example. If direct effect = -0.5 and indirect = 2.0, total = 1.5, proportion mediated = 2.0/1.5 = 133%. That's meaningless. Report the raw estimates and their CIs. Effect Size & Power

Quantifying the Magnitude of Conditional Effects

  • R² for each equation: Report R² for the M equation and Y equation separately. Journals expect this. The change in R² when adding the interaction term (ΔR²) is particularly important — it tells you how much variance the moderation adds beyond the main effects.
  • f² for interaction terms: Cohen’s f² = R²_full − R²_reduced / (1 − R²_full). Small = .02, Medium = .15, Large = .35. Interaction effects are typically small (f² = .01–.05) — be realistic.
  • For indirect effects: κ² (kappa-squared): A standardized effect size for indirect effects ranging 0–1, proposed by Preacher & Kelley (2011). Available in PROCESS output. Small ≈ .01, Medium ≈ .09, Large ≈ .25.
  • Conditional R²: Some software (lavaan, brms) provides conditional R² that accounts for random effects — preferred in multilevel extensions of PROCESS models.
Cohen’s f² for interaction term (ΔR² metric)

f² = (R²full − R²no interaction) / (1 − R²full)

Power Reality Check
Interaction effects in non-experimental social science research are typically small. Plan for f² = .01–.03 in your power analysis. At f² = .02 with N = 300, power is approximately 70%. You need large samples to reliably detect moderation.
Key Rule
Statistical significance ≠ practical importance. Always pair a significant interaction with an effect size. A significant but tiny f² may not warrant different interventions by subgroup.
Effect sizes are increasingly required by journals (APA Publication Manual, 7th ed., requires effect sizes for all key results). Walk through each metric. R² students already know. ΔR² is new — it's the most intuitive: "adding the interaction explained an additional 3% of variance in PTSS beyond what sex and violence exposure alone explained." f² computation: model without interaction first (X + W only), record R². Add interaction (X + W + X*W), record new R². Compute f² = delta R² / (1 - R²_full). Most students will have never computed this. Worth doing an in-class example. kappa-squared (κ²): This is Hayes-specific. In PROCESS output look for "Effect size(s) for indirect effect(s)". The κ² is Preacher & Kelley (2011) — it's the ratio of the obtained indirect effect to the maximum possible indirect effect given the variances. More interpretable than a raw indirect effect when comparing across studies. The power reality check is important: tell students that detecting a sex × violence interaction in community samples requires large N. This is not a methodological weakness — it's the nature of moderation. Pre-register your power analysis with G*Power or pwr2ppl package in R.

APA Results Write-Up Templates: Model by Model

Model 7 Template
“A first-stage moderated mediation model (Hayes Model 7) was estimated with [X] predicting [Y] through [M], moderated by [W]. The interaction between [X] and [W] on [M] was significant (a₃ = [value], p = [p], 95% CI [LL, UL]), indicating that the effect of [X] on [M] varied as a function of [W]. Conditional indirect effects were significant at low W (effect = [value], 95% BootCI [LL, UL]), average W (effect = [value], 95% BootCI [LL, UL]), and high W (effect = [value], 95% BootCI [LL, UL]). The index of moderated mediation was [value], 95% CI [LL, UL], confirming that the indirect effect varied significantly across levels of [W].”
Model 59 Template (addition for quadratic)
“The indirect effect of [X] on [Y] through [M] was a quadratic function of [W], as both first-stage (a₃ = [value], p < .001) and second-stage (b₃ = [value], p < .001) interactions were significant. Conditional indirect effects accelerated across levels of [W]: at low W, effect = [value] (BootCI [LL, UL]); at average W, effect = [value] (BootCI [LL, UL]); at high W, effect = [value] (BootCI [LL, UL]). This acceleration reflects the multiplicative nature of simultaneous first- and second-stage moderation.”
J-N Reporting Add-On
“Johnson-Neyman analysis indicated that the effect of [X] on [Y] was significant for values of [W] above [J-N value] (representing [X]% of the sample). Below this threshold, the effect was non-significant.”
Walk through each template sentence by sentence, explaining what goes where. These templates are based on published papers using PROCESS models and Hayes (2022). Students should modify them — not copy verbatim — but knowing the structure is invaluable. Key structural rule for results sections: (1) State the model, (2) Report whether the interaction(s) is significant and what it means, (3) Give conditional indirect effects with CIs, (4) Give the IMM with CI, (5) Translate into plain language for the substantive audience. Common student error: burying the IMM in a footnote or omitting it. Emphasize: the IMM is the HEADLINE of a moderated mediation results section. J-N reporting add-on: stress that the J-N value must be placed in context. Saying "significant above W = 2.14" is useless without also saying "this corresponds to approximately the 30th percentile of parent support in our sample, meaning 70% of participants showed a significant effect." ALWAYS convert the J-N threshold into a percentage of the sample. Encourage students to read published papers that use PROCESS Models 7, 8, or 59 in their substantive area and compare those write-ups to these templates. Journals differ in detail requirements.
